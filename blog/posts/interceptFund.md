# Regulation is all you need

The Intercept Fund has the right goal. I just think they are focusing too much on technology, and not enough on the thing that will decide whether any of that technology is actually used: regulation. If you have not read about their approach I suggest you take a look at <a href="https://www.interceptfund.com/" target="\_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">their website</a> before reading this Essay.

## Before I start

This is a critique, and in a few places it is blunt. So let me be clear about where I am coming from.

The Intercept Fund is going after the right goal. They are doing something almost nobody else has dared to do: putting serious money behind clean indoor air and the reduction of respiratory infections. That is excellent, and I want to say so plainly before I start picking holes.

My problem is not really with what they want to fund. It is that the world they are funding into lacks the preconditions for their approach to be impactful. In a different world, their plan would be close to perfect. Imagine indoor air was already regulated, or that a handful of other funders were already doing the slow groundwork I describe below. Then I would have very little to complain about. But that world does not exist yet. And because Intercept is a first mover, laudably so, nobody else is currently building it at the scale required.

Someone has to lay the foundations before all the existing and emerging technology will actually be pulled into use.

So yes, I do think the approach is not ideal, but only for the situation we are actually in. Everything on their list is worth doing. But technology will only be impactful once regulation, public awareness, standards, and institutional demand exist to pull it into the real world.

One caveat before I go on: if Intercept has information that other emerging initiatives are already pushing the regulation and awareness side, and simply have not gone public yet, then my critique falls away, same holds true if the intercept is planning to do way more on the policy side of things then their website and blogpost reveals. I would be very glad if everything that follows were obsolete.

## Technology Is Not the Bottleneck

Intercept launched with $500 million and a very clear aim: push respiratory infections down by cleaning indoor air and developing broad-spectrum preventatives. Indoor air has been ignored for decades, so I am genuinely glad to see serious money pointed at it.

But there is one thing I have to push back on, because I think it decides whether this fund changes anything at all or quietly fizzles out.

What struck me while reading their launch essay is that they have already made most of my argument for me. They say themselves that much of the air-cleaning technology already exists. Far-UVC is a bit less well studied but even there it probably is less an issue of needing new technology and just doing larger studies about safety and efficacy. Filtration works and is harmless and Ventilation of course as well. They list the real obstacles as low awareness, imperfect real-world evidence, weak standards, unclear product evaluation, and the lack of a strong demand signal. And they say, almost in passing, that broad uptake of air-cleaning technologies will ultimately come from policies that require them in high-transmission environments.

I agree with almost all of that. Which is exactly why the allocation of their attention puzzles me.

If the technology is already there, then technology is not the bottleneck. Implementation, or in their terms demand, is lacking. And demand is missing for a very specific reason: clean indoor air has all conditions for a textbook market failure. No product, however elegant, can repair that on its own. I will expand on the market failures in the next chapter.

I model energy markets and work on decarbonisation pathways aimed at guiding policy for a living. Two lessons from that world have stayed with me. The first is that a short, well-aimed policy paper can move billions and change what an entire country decides to do. The second is that gigantic sums, from companies and governments alike, can be poured into perfectly good technology and achieve very little, simply because the correct surrounding conditions were never put in place.

So let me be blunt. Funding more air-cleaning technology while the policy around it is missing is, in climate terms, like funding direct air capture in a fictive world with no carbon price, no climate targets, no clean-energy mandates, and no support even for wind or solar. In such a world you can de-risk the technology beautifully, but nobody will use it anyways.

There is also a timing issue. The equity side of the fund is betting on broad-spectrum preventatives that should reach Phase 2 trials in about three to four years. That means Phase 3 and actual rollout are years further out again. Meanwhile, the things that clean the air in a room are already here and need no drug-development pipeline. Ventilation and filtration are not medicines. They are infrastructure and large scale improvements can start instantly once regulation enables it.

## Bad Indoor Air is a result of Market Failures

Let me name the market failures, because naming them matters.

Bad indoor air is an externality. When a school, office, restaurant, shop, or train operator saves money by under-ventilating, skipping filtration, or ignoring air quality, it does not bear the full cost of that decision. The cost is pushed onto everyone else: the people who get infected, the employers who lose workdays, the health systems that treat avoidable illness, the children who learn less well, the families who absorb the disruption, and society as a whole.

At the same time, the benefits of clean air are dispersed. The person who would have avoided infection may never know. The employer may only capture part of the productivity gain. The building owner may pay for the upgrade while tenants, workers, visitors, and the public reap most of the benefit. This is both a split-incentive problem and a public-good problem. The party that has to pay is often not the party that benefits most.

So, predictably, they under-invest.

That is why regulation is not some optional add-on. It is the mechanism that makes the economics line up with the public interest. Without it, every individual actor is encouraged to free-ride on everyone else. With it, clean air becomes a normal condition of operating a building, not a voluntary luxury for unusually enlightened institutions.

There is another problem: you cannot see the thing you are trying to avoid. Infectious respiratory particles have no taste, no smell, no colour. There is no label on the door and no price tag attached to bad air. People will not reliably pay for something they cannot perceive, especially when they have no easy way to distinguish good air from bad air or good products from bad products. Intercept admits this themselves: customers cannot currently tell which products are safe, effective, or worth buying.

Then there is the historical hurdle: the droplet dogma. For decades, public health treated respiratory infection primarily as a close-contact, droplet, handwashing, and surface-contamination problem. Airborne transmission was pushed to the margins, despite being central to how many respiratory infections spread. That distorted research priorities, building standards, public messaging, and institutional responsibility. If the dominant story is "wash your hands" and "stay home when sick," then the building itself disappears from view. The air becomes nobody's job.

No enforced standards, almost no monitoring, and nobody to blame. That is the core problem. With water and food we have enforceable limits and someone who is responsible when they are broken. With indoor air we mostly have guidance, voluntary standards, and vibes. No accountability means no real market.

And finally: nobody is pushing for change at the required scale. Climate and outdoor air have Greenpeace, WWF, the Environmental Defense Fund, ClientEarth, Agora Energiewende, and many other policy shops, campaigners, litigators, and lobbyists. Indoor air has almost none of that. No large campaigning group. Barely a think tank ecosystem. No serious lobby keeping governments honest. Even the bigger players in this field survive on tiny budgets, and below them sit grassroots groups with basically nothing. It feels a lot like the climate movement in the 1980s: scientifically right, politically underpowered, and institutionally homeless.

That last gap is the one Intercept's plan does not really touch. And it is the gap that would unlock everything else.

## Clean water is the right analogy

The clean-water analogy is powerful, but I think Intercept draws the wrong lesson from it.

Clean water did not become normal because every building, restaurant, or school independently decided that cholera prevention had a positive ROI. It became normal because societies built infrastructure, created public-health institutions, introduced standards, monitored compliance, and made unsafe water unacceptable.

Nobody waited for every pipe, pump, filtration system, and disinfection method to prove its effect on disease burden through modern clinical trials in every real-world use case before acting. They used engineering, epidemiology, public-health judgment, and regulation. They treated water as shared infrastructure, not as a consumer product that needed to win a market one buyer at a time.

Indoor air should be treated the same way.

This is also where Intercept's advisory structure matters. Their advisory board includes brilliant people, and there are important exceptions. But the centre of gravity is still pharma, medicine, biology, biotech, infectious disease, and global-health investment. That background naturally leads you to see respiratory infection as a medical product problem: develop better interventions, generate stronger evidence, get regulatory approval, and then scale.

But clean indoor air is not only, or even mainly, a medical problem. It is also an engineering problem, a building-code problem, a labour-rights problem, a public-infrastructure problem, a consumer-protection problem, and a political-economy problem.

If you frame it medically, you ask: where is the Phase 3-equivalent evidence? In the medical paradigm, that usually means randomised controlled trials. That standard makes sense for many pharmaceutical products. A drug is given to an individual patient, inside an individual body, and the core question is whether that treated person does better or worse than an otherwise similar untreated person.

Clean air does not work like that. With infectious respiratory particles, the effect of an intervention depends on interaction, coverage, and scale. Cleaning the air in one classroom affects everyone sharing that room. Cleaning the air across a school, office, hospital, or transport network can change exposure patterns for whole groups of people. The value of the intervention is therefore partly collective.

In technical terms, the no-interference part of SUTVA — the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption — often does not hold. SUTVA basically says that one person's outcome should not depend on whether someone else receives the treatment. That assumption is already strained for vaccines and other infectious-disease interventions. For clean air, it is even more obviously strained, because the whole point is to change a shared environment. This does not make evaluation impossible, but it does mean that the individual-patient RCT is the wrong mental default.

If you frame clean air as civil infrastructure, you ask a different question: what does it cost, what risks does it reduce, what co-benefits does it create, which buildings should be prioritised, who pays, who benefits, and what is the expected value under realistic uncertainty? That is how societies already decide on transport systems, energy infrastructure, flood protection, building codes, and public-health infrastructure. They do not wait for a Phase 3-equivalent trial of every bridge, railway, grid expansion, insulation programme, or drinking-water upgrade. They use engineering evidence, physics, observational data, modelling, pilots, monitoring, and cost-benefit analysis.

But even cost-benefit analysis is not the whole frame. Clean air is also about rights, equity, and distribution. People do not choose the air in most of the places where they spend their lives. Children do not choose their classrooms. Workers often cannot choose their offices, factories, shops, or care settings. Patients cannot choose the air in a waiting room. Commuters cannot choose the ventilation standard of a train carriage. The people most exposed are often the people with the least power to demand better conditions.

That matters. A purely aggregate cost-benefit calculation can hide who bears the risk and who captures the benefit. Clean indoor air should not become a premium feature for wealthy schools, high-end offices, private clinics, and expensive restaurants while everyone else is left with stale air and voluntary guidance. If the benefits are public, the protections should be public too.

So yes, evidence matters. Of course we should measure. Of course we should study real-world performance. Of course we should avoid snake oil. Far-UVC and antimicrobial vapours deserve particular scrutiny because they involve active technologies and safety questions. But the general idea that public buildings should have enough clean air is not a speculative biomedical hypothesis. It is basic infrastructure.

The right standard is not: has every outcome been proven beyond clinical doubt? The right standard is closer to: is this a low-risk intervention with plausible mechanisms, measurable delivery, multiple lines of supporting evidence, positive expected value under reasonable assumptions, and a strong equity case for public action?

That is a normal infrastructure question, but also a normal public-interest question. Cost-benefit analysis always contains uncertainty. Energy-system models contain uncertainty. Transport-demand models contain uncertainty. Climate-adaptation models contain uncertainty. But governments still use them, because the alternative is not certainty. The alternative is pretending that doing nothing is neutral, when it also has costs, and those costs are not evenly distributed.

Bringing most public buildings up to sensible ventilation and filtration levels is not wildly expensive compared with the social cost of respiratory disease, poor concentration, sick days, reduced learning, and pandemic vulnerability. Even if you stay cautious about the disease numbers, the ordinary benefits like better concentration, fewer headaches, fewer sick days, better learning, and greater resilience during heat waves and smoke events already justify action. The equity argument makes that case stronger, not weaker.

A government can behave here like a venture investor: back a portfolio of low-risk interventions with positive expected value, accept that some effects will be smaller than hoped, and still come out well ahead. But it should also behave like a government: protect people in the shared spaces where they cannot protect themselves, set minimum standards, and make sure the benefits of clean air do not depend on income, bargaining power, or postcode. You do not need absolute clinical proof to justify something cheap, useful, measurable, mostly harmless, and unequally needed. Demanding that level of certainty creates its own harm, because every year of delay has an opportunity cost.

## Where some of the money should go instead

Alongside the new technologies Intercept wants to push, spending a meaningful share of the money on the unglamorous things that actually enable these new technologies as well as the existing solutions could greatly increase the impact.

Awareness work that makes invisible air visible, and turns clean air into something people simply expect.

Lobbying for policy and standards, so that ventilation and filtration targets become monitored and enforced values rather than paperweights.

Techno-economic studies based on technologies that already exist. You do not need novel far-UVC to hit a sensible clean-air target in many buildings. Ordinary ventilation, filtration, CO2 monitoring, and maintenance get you a long way. That case needs to be put cleanly in front of regulators.

One reason this should be tractable is that the counterfactual already exists. In almost every building category, there are already buildings with decent indoor air, and buildings with very poor indoor air, often serving almost identical purposes. Some schools have acceptable CO2 levels; others regularly drift into concentrations that make clear how under-ventilated they are. The same is true for doctors' offices, waiting rooms, public offices, shops, restaurants, and trains. That variation matters. It shows that clean indoor air is not some exotic technical fantasy. It is already being achieved in many ordinary buildings. The problem is that it is uneven, invisible, and not mandatory.

That also makes it very unlikely that the economic costs are inherently prohibitive. If one school can provide acceptable air while another similar school cannot, the question is not whether it is technically possible. The question is why the second building is allowed to fall so far behind, what upgrade pathway would fix it, and who should pay. Schools and doctors' offices should be obvious high-priority categories: they are high-contact environments, they include vulnerable people, and in many countries they are exactly the kind of places where CO2 readings can be shockingly high.

Nobody has yet properly calculated what a nationwide target like 800 ppm CO2 in non-residential buildings, combined with filtration where needed, would cost financially and energetically. Nobody has mapped properly which actors would have to pay, how upgrades could be subsidised, how costs could be recovered, and which building categories should be prioritised first.

These open questions are where policy papers by think tanks shine. They can provide rough estimates in a language understandable by politicians, journalists and interested persons of the civil society, igniting a wide discussion about the topic in the society.

Support for the small civil-society groups already carrying this work, who are chronically underfunded.

And yes, lobbying. I know that word makes many people uncomfortable, but it should not. The tobacco industry lobbied. The fossil-fuel industry lobbied. The weapons industry lobbies. They understood something very simple: changing the rules of the game is often far more cost-effective than improving the product. Fossil-fuel companies did not merely try to make better oil. They shaped regulation, delayed constraints, influenced public debate, and protected demand. Tobacco companies did not merely compete on product quality. They fought warning labels, taxes, advertising restrictions, liability, and public-health rules.

For once, why not use that lesson for something good?

If lobbying is powerful enough to delay climate policy, protect cigarettes, and sustain harmful industries, then surely it is powerful enough to help children breathe better air in classrooms. A tiny fraction of Intercept's budget spent on serious policy advocacy, model legislation, public campaigns, standards work, and institutional coalition-building could move far more money than it directly spends.

That is not a distraction from deployment. It is what makes deployment possible.

## The missing piece

A customer advisory board with large companies is useful. It can help identify buyer needs, improve product design, and create early demand. But it is not a substitute for regulation.

The highest-value environments for clean-air intervention are not necessarily the places with the most sophisticated buyers. They are schools, public transport, hospitals, care homes, shelters, public offices, crowded workplaces, and other spaces where people cannot simply opt out. Many of these do not behave like normal consumer markets. They are budget-constrained, politically governed, and slow-moving. If you want them to change, you need standards, procurement rules, subsidies, inspection regimes, and public pressure.

In other words, you need politics.

This is where philanthropy can be uniquely useful. Companies are not going to fund the creation of strong ventilation laws unless those laws obviously benefit them. Governments often move slowly until someone makes the case unavoidable. Grassroots groups have the moral argument but not the resources. Philanthropy can fill that gap.

That is the missing demand engine.

## Final Thoughts

I want Intercept to succeed. I really do. Their goal is right, their ambition is rare, and the field desperately needs more serious actors.

But if the plan is mostly to de-risk products, generate more evidence, and hope customers and regulators later pull the technologies into the world, then I think they are underestimating the central problem.

The main bottleneck is not that humanity lacks air-cleaning tools. The main bottleneck is that nobody is required to care.

That is why regulation is all you need, not literally, of course, but strategically. Regulation creates the market. Regulation forces monitoring. Regulation assigns responsibility. Regulation gives good suppliers a reason to scale. Regulation turns clean air from a nice-to-have into a basic expectation.

For what it is worth, I built IndoorCO2Map, a citizen-science platform that maps indoor CO2 in public spaces, in my spare time and with no budget at all. I will say this plainly, even if it sounds arrogant: if Intercept sticks too closely to the path it has laid out, I think there is a real chance that small, scrappy community projects like this end up doing more to shift public expectations around indoor air than the Intercept Fund does.

I would much rather be wrong about that.

But if I am not, the reason will be simple: technology does not deploy itself. Markets do not appear by magic. And clean air, like clean water, will not become normal until society decides to make it normal.
